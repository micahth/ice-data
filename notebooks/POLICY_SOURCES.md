# Economic rationale: policy claims and the association being tested

These primary sources document what Trump and administration officials **claimed**, not whether those claims are true. They motivate asking whether enforcement intensity accompanies better labor-market/economic outcomes. Most concern wages, jobs or fiscal burdens, **not total GDP**; legal-immigration restrictions are not deportation of unauthorized workers.

The later Treasury statement postdates the analysis's March 2026 event cutoff: it documents continuing rhetoric, **not an ex-ante hypothesis or a policy event in the sample**. The source set is illustrative rather than exhaustive. Statements were located in official indexed transcripts/releases; no cost estimates or self-deportation totals are accepted as established findings here.

## Operational hypothesis and competing mechanisms

If unauthorized workers are close substitutes for other workers, actual removals could reduce labor competition and eventually improve the remaining workers' employment or wages. Under that mechanism, greater exposure might accompany lower unemployment or higher wages **after adjustment**. Wage, job-quality, sector and fiscal outcomes would test these claims more directly than total output; this dataset does not include them.

Removing workers also reduces production capacity and consumer demand, may disrupt complementary jobs and can impose enforcement costs. Consequently, **higher aggregate GDP growth is not a necessary prediction** of a wage-benefit argument. GDP, per-capita income, worker wages and public budgets are different estimands. Economic improvement after removals would not, by itself, establish causation.

The implemented exploratory comparison is:

`annual state outcome change ~ contemporaneous arrests per 100k + lagged outcome + year effects (when applicable)`

GDP growth and unemployment change are economic outcomes; violent/property crime changes address a separate public-safety question. The reduced-control model is descriptive; broader controls and an influential-state exclusion assess model sensitivity.

**Arrests are an imperfect enforcement proxy, not a workforce-removal treatment.** Arrested people need not be employed, reside in the apprehension state or be deported that year; voluntary departures are not counted. GDP and enforcement can respond to the same state conditions, and enforcement may target changing conditions. A same-year state correlation cannot distinguish removal effects, targeting, common shocks or delayed adjustment.

This is a retrospective exploratory design, not a preregistered test of administration policy. Confidence intervals quantify sampling/model uncertainty conditional on the chosen specification; they do not repair treatment mismeasurement or confounding. The report therefore neither endorses the administration's claims nor interprets a null association as evidence that deportation has no economic consequences.
