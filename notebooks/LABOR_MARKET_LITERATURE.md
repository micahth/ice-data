# Labor-market literature and research motivation

Research on immigration enforcement finds effects that are not captured by a simple labor-competition account. East, Hines, Luck, Mansour and Velásquez study Secure Communities, a police-based enforcement policy implemented from 2008 to 2013. Using variation in implementation across local areas and over time, they find decreases in employment among likely undocumented immigrants and U.S.-born people, as well as lower hourly wages for U.S.-born workers. They discuss higher labor costs and reduced local consumption as possible mechanisms.

> East, Chloe N., Annie L. Hines, Philip Luck, Hani Mansour, and Andrea Velásquez. 2023. “The Labor Market Effects of Immigration Enforcement.” *Journal of Labor Economics* 41(4): 957–996. [https://doi.org/10.1086/721152](https://doi.org/10.1086/721152).

That study and this project examine different interventions, periods, units and outcomes. Secure Communities was a local-area policy with staggered implementation; this project uses annual state arrest rates as an imperfect proxy for enforcement exposure. Arrests do not establish that someone was employed, removed from the workforce or deported. The current analysis is descriptive and cannot identify the causal labor-market effects of enforcement.

Labor supply and demand, production complementarities, consumer demand, enforcement targeting and adjustment over time can all shape observed outcomes. Wage and employment measures would address labor-market claims more directly than aggregate GDP; those measures are not available in these data. Crime outcomes are a separate question and are not implied by labor-market findings.
